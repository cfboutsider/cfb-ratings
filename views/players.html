Player Board

Every player's season

Three numbers per player, and they are three different kinds of claim. Stats are counted from play-by-play. Rating is modelled. Stars are a translation of the rating, and the weakest of the three. Rating is opponent-adjusted and shrunk toward the role’s own median by how well that role predicts itself — a modelled measurement rather than a counted one. Stars place that rating on the recruiting axis: they answer “what would he be called if he entered the portal today”, which is a question about language rather than about the player.

Transferring is a column here, not the entry ticket. A transfer happens after a season is played, so it is flagged onto the season it is read from rather than the season it belongs to. Where a player went is shown; nothing about the move changes the numbers beside it.

“Team” is what his own teammates did at the same job, leave-one-out, so a back taking most of the carries is not compared to himself; vs Tm is the gap. It is shown beside the rate and never subtracted from it. That restraint is measured rather than cautious: building the rating on the gap instead of the rate collapses out-of-sample prediction of a transfer at his next school from R² 0.283 to 0.024. The team a player leaves predicts his production at his new school about as well as his own rate does, so removing it removes information about him. The column answers “was it him or the offence around him”, which is a question about description, not prediction. Harding’s 2025 backfield is the case that prompted it: 14.35 yards a carry beside teammates at 7.87, on a team that led all 667 in the corpus at 8.73.

“After the season” answers one question with four different kinds of certainty, and a player who disappears but was not a senior is called “off rosters”, never graduated. A destination is a fact — he is in next year’s incoming class with a school attached. Returned and graduated are read from whether he is on a roster the following season: absence plus eligibility running out is about as close to graduation as this data gets. A sophomore who vanishes is far more likely a transfer to a school we did not scrape than a man who finished his degree two years early, and naming the two the same would turn a coverage gap into a life event. A blank means he was not on a roster we hold for this season either, so nothing can be said.

The Rating column holds two different measurements, marked 0.8536O for offensive production and 0.8536D for defensive. The defensive rating is not comparable across divisions. O is per-attempt production, opponent-adjusted through the division bridge, so a dominant D3 quarterback is not mistaken for an FBS one. D is given that he made the tackle, how good was the tackle, rated inside his own position group. A defender has no denominator — his credited snaps are his tackles — so volume is deliberately not in the rating, only in how far it is shrunk. The division warning is measured rather than assumed: a safety makes his tackle about 8.5 yards downfield whether he plays for Ohio State or Oberlin (D2 8.37, D3 8.73, FBS 8.52, FCS 8.47). Division shows up in how often a defence gets a stop, not in the geometry of the tackles that happen.

A blank rating means one of two things, and the page counts them rather than guessing: No defensive production rating has been built for the roles that show a blank, so a blank is the correct output for a thing that does not exist — inventing one to fill the column would be inventing a finding. A rating is also withheld where the rated role covers under 40% of a player’s snaps: a punter’s rating on a quarterback’s line is real, and about the wrong thing.

Nothing on this page is read by any rating. Swapping production into the talent composite was measured at −0.0061 MAE, CI [−0.035, +0.024] — nominally the wrong way. The event counts behind these rows are not inert: they feed net returning production, summed per team. So a team’s rating can move on how much a player played, never on the number shown here.
